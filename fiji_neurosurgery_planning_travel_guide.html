The existing Fiji neurosurgery series already explains clinical decision-making, costs, surgeon and hospital selection, technology and the most important FAQs. This companion guide focuses on the remaining practical questions: preparing before travel, organising the Fiji-to-India journey, planning an attendant and accommodation, preparing for discharge, flying home safely, rehabilitation in Fiji, warning signs, emergency planning and continuity of care.

1. Quick Planning Framework for Fiji Patients

A successful medical journey should be planned as one continuous pathway rather than as an operation followed by a flight home. The practical sequence is:

  • Collect complete medical records and actual MRI/CT/angiogram images.
  • Obtain a procedure-specific specialist opinion from India before committing to travel.
  • Clarify whether surgery is necessary, what procedure is proposed and whether a radiosurgical or endovascular alternative exists.
  • Shortlist two or three relevant surgeon-hospital combinations rather than collecting many unrelated quotations.
  • Obtain a written estimate with technology, ICU stay and exclusions clearly identified.
  • Plan travel documents, flights, an attendant where appropriate, accommodation and a contingency reserve.
  • Allow time in India for examination, final investigations, surgery, recovery, surgeon review and fit-to-fly clearance.
  • Leave India with complete medical records, rehabilitation instructions and a defined Fiji follow-up pathway.
The Key Principle For Fiji patients, the medical journey does not end at hospital discharge in India. A strong treatment plan includes the return flight and safe continuity of care after the patient reaches Fiji.

2. Pre-Surgery Preparation Before Leaving Fiji

Medical Preparation

Before travel, the Indian treating team should receive enough information to understand both the neurological problem and the patient’s general medical condition. The exact preoperative requirements vary by procedure and patient.

  • Current symptoms, duration and neurological findings.
  • MRI report plus the actual MRI images; CT and angiogram images where available.
  • Previous neurosurgical records and older imaging when relevant.
  • Current medicines, allergies and major medical conditions.
  • Relevant recent blood tests and other investigations requested by the treating team.
  • Histopathology records for tumours where applicable.

Medication and Medical-Condition Review

Patients should ask the treating team how medicines such as blood thinners, anti-seizure medication and other prescription drugs should be managed around surgery. These decisions must be individualised; medicines should not be stopped solely on the basis of a general travel guide.

Chronic conditions such as diabetes, hypertension or heart disease can influence anaesthesia, wound healing, infection risk and recovery. Complex patients may need additional medical or anaesthesia review before a final surgical plan is confirmed.

Prepare Your Fiji Home Before You Travel

  • Arrange help with meals, transport and household tasks for the early recovery period.
  • Reduce avoidable stairs or obstacles where practical.
  • Plan where the patient will rest safely, particularly if seizure precautions are relevant.
  • Identify the Fiji neurologist or physiotherapist who may assist after return.
  • Discuss how postoperative imaging can be shared with the Indian team.

3. Fiji-to-India Travel, Documents and Practical Preparation

Travel and entry requirements can change. Fiji patients should verify the current Indian visa/entry requirements and airline rules at the time of booking rather than relying on an older guide.

Documents to Keep Together

  • Passport and current travel/visa documents as applicable.
  • Hospital invitation or appointment documents where provided.
  • Medical summary, imaging reports and digital imaging files.
  • Medication list and adequate essential medicines for the journey, subject to airline and entry rules.
  • Hospital contact details, accommodation details and airport-transfer information.
  • Copies of important documents stored securely in digital form as well as the originals where appropriate.

Flight Planning Before Surgery

Patients with neurological symptoms, seizure risk or reduced mobility should consider airport wheelchair assistance and avoid unnecessarily difficult connections where possible. The hospital should know the planned arrival date so that consultation, evaluation and accommodation can be coordinated.

Do Not Make the Return Flight Too Rigid Being discharged from hospital and being fit for long-distance air travel are not the same thing. A flexible return itinerary is safer for major neurosurgery because wound healing, neurological recovery and medical clearance can vary.

4. Should an Attendant Travel from Fiji?

For major neurosurgery, an accompanying family member or attendant is often practical. The attendant can help with admission, communication, belongings, medicines, meals, mobility after discharge, follow-up visits, airport movement and luggage.

Budget for the Attendant

  • Fiji-India airfare.
  • Accommodation and meals.
  • Local transport.
  • Possible extended stay if the patient’s recovery takes longer than expected.
  • Changes to return flights where necessary.

For a straightforward, limited procedure in an independent patient, the level of assistance required may be lower. For major tumour, vascular or complex surgery, or where neurological deficits are present, support becomes more important.

5. What Happens After Arrival in India?

A remote opinion is usually preliminary. A typical pathway after arrival is: specialist consultation → physical and neurological examination → review of imaging → additional investigations if needed → final diagnosis → final surgical recommendation → anaesthesia/medical clearance → final quotation → admission → surgery.

The final procedure or cost can change if examination or new investigations reveal information that was not apparent during remote review. Patients should therefore understand that an initial quotation is not always the final bill.

Before Signing Consent, Clarify:

  • The exact diagnosis and which finding explains the symptoms.
  • Why surgery is recommended and what happens if it is delayed.
  • The exact procedure and whether a radiosurgical or endovascular option was considered.
  • Technology such as neuronavigation or intraoperative monitoring proposed.
  • Major risks that are particularly relevant to the individual patient.
  • Expected hospital and ICU stay and estimated total India stay.
  • The postoperative plan and anticipated fit-to-fly review.

6. Accommodation After Hospital Discharge in India

Patients do not necessarily need to remain hospitalised for the entire recovery period. When medically appropriate, a nearby hotel or serviced apartment may be suitable.

Features Worth Prioritising

  • Elevator access and minimal stairs.
  • Accessible bathroom and a suitable bed.
  • Easy transport back to the treating hospital.
  • Nearby pharmacy and appropriate meals.
  • Space for the attendant.
  • Reasonable distance from the hospital rather than choosing only by lowest room price.
Medical Value Applies to Accommodation Too For a recently operated neurosurgical patient, accessible accommodation close to the hospital can be more useful than a cheaper property that creates difficult travel, stairs or long journeys for follow-up.

7. Discharge Readiness: Questions Before Leaving the Hospital

Before leaving the hospital to local recovery accommodation, clarify the following with the surgical team:

  • Is the wound satisfactory and what wound care is required?
  • What medicines should be taken, for how long, and which medicines should be avoided?
  • How much activity is appropriate?
  • Are there restrictions on driving, bathing or specific movements?
  • When should sutures or staples be removed if applicable?
  • What symptoms require urgent review?
  • When is the next surgeon review?
  • What imaging will be required before or after returning to Fiji?
  • When will the patient be assessed for fitness to fly?

8. Flying Back to Fiji After Neurosurgery

There is no universal fit-to-fly interval after neurosurgery. Timing depends on the procedure, wound healing, neurological stability, seizure risk and the risk of complications.

Long-Distance Flight Planning

  • Obtain explicit fit-to-fly guidance from the treating surgeon.
  • Ask about seizure precautions during travel if relevant.
  • Maintain hydration as advised.
  • Discuss individualised blood-clot prevention where appropriate.
  • Plan medication timing across the long journey, including anti-seizure medication if prescribed.
  • Arrange wheelchair assistance if walking through large airports will be difficult.
  • Discuss suitable seating and luggage restrictions.
  • Keep essential medicines and important medical documents accessible during travel.
Important Hospital discharge should never be treated as automatic clearance for immediate international travel. Fiji patients should allow enough time in India for recovery and medical review before the long journey home.

9. The Medical Records Fiji Patients Should Bring Home

  • Final discharge summary.
  • Operative report or operative notes.
  • Final diagnosis.
  • Relevant preoperative and postoperative imaging.
  • Histopathology where relevant.
  • Medication prescription and duration.
  • Rehabilitation and activity instructions.
  • Wound-care instructions.
  • Fit-to-fly advice where required.
  • Follow-up schedule and timing of future imaging.
  • Emergency warning signs.
  • Direct postoperative contact or remote-review pathway with the Indian treating team.

These records allow the Fiji neurologist or doctor to understand exactly what was done in India and reduce avoidable gaps in continuity of care.

10. Recovery in Fiji: A Practical Phase-by-Phase Framework

Recovery varies considerably between endoscopic pituitary surgery, craniotomy for tumours, vascular surgery, radiosurgery and complex or paediatric procedures. The following framework is deliberately general and must be replaced by the patient’s own surgeon-specific instructions.

Phase 1: Early Phase After Return

  • Follow wound-care and medication instructions exactly.
  • Continue any prescribed seizure precautions.
  • Watch for new neurological symptoms, wound changes, fever or other warning signs.
  • Arrange the planned local medical or physiotherapy review.
  • Avoid assuming that feeling better means all restrictions can immediately be stopped.

Phase 2: Intermediate Recovery

  • Progress activity according to the operation and surgeon’s protocol.
  • Begin or advance rehabilitation when prescribed.
  • Review pain medicines and other postoperative medication.
  • Complete scheduled imaging.
  • Discuss driving, work and exercise before resuming them.

Phase 3: Longer-Term Recovery

  • Tumour and vascular procedures may require longer-term clinical and imaging surveillance.
  • Rehabilitation should be adjusted to the procedure and the patient’s neurological recovery.
  • Return to physically demanding work may take substantially longer than return to desk-based activity.
  • Persistent or worsening symptoms should be reassessed rather than simply attributed to ‘normal recovery.’

11. What If Something Goes Wrong After Returning to Fiji?

This question should be answered before surgery. A practical international-patient plan is: Indian surgeon + Fiji doctor/neurologist + remote review, with a clear understanding of which problems require local emergency care.

Potential Warning Signs That Require Prompt Medical Assessment:

  • New or worsening weakness, numbness, vision change or speech difficulty.
  • Seizures, particularly if new or different from before.
  • Severe or rapidly worsening headache.
  • Fever, wound redness, increasing swelling, drainage or wound separation.
  • Sudden confusion or altered consciousness.
  • Any sudden neurological deterioration or other symptom the treating team has identified as an emergency.

For urgent symptoms, patients should seek appropriate local emergency care in Fiji rather than waiting for an overseas message response. Remote review can support continuity but cannot replace emergency assessment.

Questions to Settle Before Leaving India:

  • Who should the patient contact first if a problem develops?
  • Can the Indian surgeon review imaging and reports remotely?
  • Can the Fiji doctor communicate directly with the Indian team?
  • Which symptoms require immediate emergency assessment in Fiji?
  • Under what circumstances might return to India be considered?

12. Rehabilitation in Fiji

Rehabilitation is not identical after every neurosurgical procedure. The treating team should provide a written rehabilitation protocol that can be shared with the Fiji physiotherapist or rehabilitation team.

  • Clarify when rehabilitation should start.
  • Clarify activities that should temporarily be avoided.
  • Provide the rehabilitation team with the operative diagnosis and procedure performed.
  • Confirm any specific precautions relevant to the procedure.
  • Ask when more intensive rehabilitation can progress.
  • Use remote review if progress is unexpectedly slow or symptoms change.

13. Returning to Work, Driving and Normal Activities in Fiji

There is no single timeline for return to normal life. A patient after a limited procedure may progress differently from a patient after major tumour, vascular or complex surgery.

Discuss These Milestones Specifically:

  • Independent walking and daily activity.
  • Prolonged sitting.
  • Driving, which may have specific restrictions after neurosurgery or seizures.
  • Desk work or working from home.
  • Manual work and physical exertion.
  • Exercise and gym activity.
  • Return to activities with fall or head-injury risk.
  • Long-distance travel after the initial return to Fiji.

The safest advice is procedure-specific. Patients should obtain written guidance rather than relying on a generic internet recovery timetable, particularly regarding driving restrictions.

14. Cost Contingency and Financial Planning

Treatment cost and total medical travel cost are different. The total Fiji-to-India budget can include airfare, accommodation before and after hospitalisation, attendant expenses, meals, local transport, visa-related expenses where applicable, medicines, rehabilitation, flight changes and extended stay.

Why a Contingency Reserve Matters

Complex neurosurgery may require additional investigations, longer hospitalisation, extended ICU care, treatment of unrelated medical problems or a longer recovery period. A written quotation should therefore identify inclusions and exclusions clearly.

Three-Option Comparison

For major elective neurosurgery, comparing approximately two or three carefully selected treatment options is often more useful than collecting many quotations. Compare:

  • Proposed procedure and approach.
  • Surgeon’s experience with similar cases.
  • Hospital capability and relevant technology.
  • ICU assumptions.
  • Complete treatment estimate and exclusions.
  • Expected India stay and Fiji follow-up plan.

16. Key Takeaway for Fiji Patients

Key Takeaway Formula

Right Diagnosis + Right Procedure + Right Surgeon + Right Hospital + Appropriate Technology + Transparent Cost + Safe Travel + Planned Recovery + Reliable Follow-Up in Fiji.

The strongest neurosurgery plan is not simply one that gets the patient through an operation in India. It is one that anticipates the entire journey from Fiji, prepares for recovery, establishes safe fit-to-fly timing, transfers the medical information back home and provides a clear pathway if questions or complications arise after return.
