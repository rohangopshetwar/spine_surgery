Part 1: Clinical Decision, Need for Surgery & Surgeon Selection (Q1 – Q11)

Fiji patients may consider India for brain tumour surgery, cerebral aneurysm treatment, skull-base surgery and other complex neurosurgical procedures that are expensive, delayed, or require highly specialised expertise not readily available at home. Indian neurosurgical centres offer a broad range of techniques, including microsurgery, endoscopic approaches and navigation-guided surgery. The decision should nevertheless be based on whether the proposed treatment is genuinely appropriate for the individual patient's diagnosis, not simply because a procedure is available in India.
An abnormal scan alone does not mean that surgery is required. The decision should correlate the patient's symptoms, detailed neurological examination, the specific imaging findings, the size and location of any lesion, and whether the condition is stable or progressing. Some conditions, such as small, asymptomatic tumours or unruptured lesions with low risk, may be appropriately monitored rather than operated on immediately.
Often, yes, for a preliminary opinion. Fiji patients can send MRI, CT or angiogram images and reports along with a clinical history before travelling. A final treatment plan, however, usually requires in-person neurological examination and, in many cases, additional investigations performed in India.
For major neurosurgery, particularly involving tumours, vascular conditions, or complex reconstructive cases, a second opinion can be extremely valuable. It can clarify whether surgery is genuinely the best option, whether the timing is appropriate, and whether alternative approaches such as radiosurgery have been properly considered.
Send current symptoms and their progression, detailed neurological findings, actual MRI, CT and angiogram images along with their reports, current medications including any anti-seizure medication, and details of other medical conditions.
Options include craniotomy for brain tumours, cerebral aneurysm clipping or coiling, endoscopic pituitary surgery, skull-base surgery, and complex procedures involving the brain, spinal cord or peripheral nerves. The appropriate procedure depends heavily on the specific diagnosis, location and extent of the condition.
Not automatically. Endoscopic and minimally invasive approaches can be excellent for selected conditions such as certain pituitary tumours, but extensive or deeply located lesions may still require a more traditional open approach for safety and completeness of treatment. The right choice depends on the specific pathology, not on which technique sounds least invasive.
It can be an appropriate option for selected small tumours or certain vascular malformations, offering a non-invasive alternative to open surgery in the right circumstances. However, not every condition or lesion size is suitable for radiosurgery, and this should be discussed in detail with the neurosurgical and radiation oncology team.
It can be genuinely valuable for improving precision in complex or deep-seated lesions. However, an experienced neurosurgeon's judgement and technique remain the most important factor in outcome. Ask specifically what advantage the technology is expected to provide for your particular condition rather than assuming more advanced technology automatically means a safer operation.
There is no single 'best' neurosurgeon for every patient. Look for a surgeon with substantial, specific experience in your exact condition — for example, aneurysm surgery, skull-base tumours, or paediatric neurosurgery each require different specific expertise. Consider training, case volume for the particular pathology, and the surgeon's ability to clearly explain risks, alternatives and expected outcomes.
Independently check the surgeon's medical registration, recognised neurosurgical qualifications, subspecialty fellowship training where relevant, current hospital affiliation and any academic publications, rather than relying solely on promotional 'Top 10' rankings.

Part 2: Hospitals, City Selection, Pricing & Quotations (Q12 – Q20)

Consider the hospital's neurosurgical infrastructure, including a dedicated neuro-ICU, advanced imaging, intraoperative navigation, neuromonitoring capability, and multidisciplinary support involving neurology, radiation oncology and rehabilitation where relevant.
Accreditation is a useful indicator of organisational and safety standards, but it should be combined with direct evaluation of the surgeon's specific experience and the hospital's neurosurgical and critical-care infrastructure.
Major neurosurgical programmes exist in Delhi NCR, Mumbai, Chennai, Bengaluru and Hyderabad. Fiji patients should select the surgeon and hospital first, based on their specific condition, and treat the city as a secondary consideration.
Costs vary substantially by procedure and complexity. A relatively limited procedure will generally cost less than complex tumour resection, aneurysm surgery, or skull-base reconstruction, which require longer operative time, specialised equipment and extended ICU care. These figures should be treated as broad planning ranges rather than fixed quotations.
Important factors include the specific procedure, the technology used such as navigation or intraoperative monitoring, the length of ICU stay required, and the patient's overall medical complexity, including any need for further treatment such as radiotherapy.
Request a written, itemised quotation covering the surgeon's fee, operating theatre charges, the ICU stay, a standard room, routine investigations and an agreed total hospital stay. Do not assume an item is included simply because a package price is quoted.
Possible exclusions include additional ICU days, treatment of complications, further treatment such as radiotherapy if needed, outpatient rehabilitation after discharge, and accommodation for an attendant.
Compare the same diagnosis, the same proposed procedure, the same technology assumptions, the same expected ICU and hospital stay, and the same inclusions and exclusions before concluding which quotation genuinely represents better value.
Price should matter only after clinical suitability, including the surgeon's specific experience and the hospital's neuro-critical-care capability, has been established.

Part 3: Budgeting, Attendants, Stay & Fit-to-Fly (Q21 – Q30)

Consider Fiji-India airfare, accommodation before and after hospitalisation, an attendant's travel and expenses, local transport, medicines, and a contingency reserve, since complex neurosurgery can sometimes involve an extended or unpredictable recovery period.
For major neurosurgery, an attendant is generally practical, particularly if the patient has any neurological deficit, to help with communication, mobility, medication reminders and follow-up appointments.
The stay should allow for thorough preoperative evaluation, surgery, a period of close postoperative monitoring, at least one surgeon review, and medical clearance for the flight home. Complex tumour, vascular or skull-base surgery generally requires a longer stay than more limited procedures.
A hotel or serviced apartment near the hospital, with easy access, minimal stairs and a safe resting environment, is generally more suitable than a cheaper property further away, particularly if any residual neurological symptoms affect balance or mobility.
There is no universal fit-to-fly date. Clearance depends on neurological stability, wound healing, seizure risk, and the absence of any ongoing complication requiring close monitoring. Hospital discharge does not automatically mean a patient is ready for a long-distance international flight.
Discuss seizure precautions if relevant, hydration, medication timing, suitable seating, and whether wheelchair assistance would be helpful, particularly for connections through large airports.
Carry the discharge summary, complete operative report, histopathology results if a tumour was removed, relevant imaging, medication list, rehabilitation instructions, the follow-up schedule, and emergency warning signs specific to the procedure performed.
Follow-up should ideally combine the Indian neurosurgeon, a Fiji doctor or neurologist, and remote review where offered, with imaging surveillance and routine monitoring coordinated between both teams.
Look for the right diagnosis confirmed through thorough investigation, the right procedure for that diagnosis, a surgeon with specific relevant experience, a hospital with appropriate neurosurgical and critical-care infrastructure, transparent cost, and a clear follow-up plan for Fiji.
No. Some small, slow-growing or asymptomatic tumours, particularly in older patients or those with significant surgical risk, are monitored with periodic imaging rather than operated on immediately. This decision depends entirely on the specific tumour type, size, location and growth pattern.

Part 4: Additional Planning, Travel, Recovery & Follow-Up (Q31 – Q41)

Ensure the Indian team has current symptoms, detailed neurological findings, actual imaging, current medications including anti-seizure medication if relevant, and any relevant blood tests well before travel.
Medicines should never be stopped on the basis of general advice. Blood thinners and anti-seizure medications, in particular, require careful individualised management by the treating team around the timing of surgery.
Arrange help with household tasks, plan a safe resting area free of fall hazards, and identify a Fiji neurologist or doctor who can provide postoperative care and monitoring.
Keep together the passport and travel documents, hospital appointment letters, medical summary, imaging reports and digital files, medication list, and accommodation and transfer details.
Patients with neurological symptoms, weakness, or reduced mobility should arrange wheelchair assistance in advance, particularly for long connections through large airports.
Yes. Hospital discharge does not automatically mean fitness for a long flight, and neurological recovery, wound healing and seizure risk can all affect the exact date of safe travel.
An attendant can help with hospital admission, communication with the medical team, mobility, medication reminders, meals, follow-up appointments and airport logistics, which can be particularly important if any neurological deficit is present.
A typical pathway includes specialist consultation, detailed neurological examination, review of imaging, any additional investigations required, confirmation of the diagnosis, the final surgical recommendation, anaesthesia clearance, a final written quotation, admission and then surgery.
Yes. In-person examination or additional investigations in India can reveal information not apparent during remote review, which can change the recommended procedure, its extent, and the final cost.
Confirm the exact diagnosis, why surgery is being recommended and what happens if it is delayed, the technology proposed such as navigation or neuromonitoring, the major risks relevant to the individual patient, the expected hospital and ICU stay, and the anticipated fit-to-fly timeline.
Ask about wound care, appropriate activity levels, any restrictions on driving, bathing or specific movements, warning symptoms requiring urgent review, the next review date, and when fitness to fly will be assessed.

Part 5: Warning Signs, Rehabilitation & Planning Checklist (Q42 – Q50)

Watch for new or worsening weakness, numbness, vision or speech difficulty, seizures particularly if new or different from before, severe or rapidly worsening headache, fever, wound changes, or sudden confusion.
Seek prompt local emergency assessment in Fiji rather than waiting for an overseas response, and use any established remote-review pathway with the Indian team for less urgent concerns.
Remote review can support continuity of care where the treating team offers it; clarify this arrangement, including how to share follow-up scans, before leaving India.
This depends heavily on the specific procedure and any neurological deficit present; obtain a written rehabilitation protocol before returning home.
Provide the operative diagnosis, procedure performed, histopathology results if relevant, and any specific precautions or restrictions recommended by the Indian surgical team.
This varies considerably by procedure, extent of surgery, and any residual neurological effects; obtain individualised, procedure-specific advice rather than a generic timeline.
Discuss driving restrictions, which may be specific after neurosurgery or seizures, along with exercise and general activity directly with the treating team.
Additional investigations, a longer ICU stay, management of an unexpected complication, or a delayed recovery can all increase costs beyond the original quotation for complex neurosurgery.
